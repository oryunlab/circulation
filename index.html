Circulatory Sensing

Eastern systems of care as cosmotechnics in participatory, embodied interaction

Oryun Lab · Ho Chi Minh City · practice-based research

Move near the circle, tap, hold, or drag to turn it

01Circulatory Sensing

Small sensing devices, made from the tools of East Asian body care, that people hold and sense through together. They read the body through touch, breath and rhythm, as Eastern medicine does, and answer in light, sound and vibration that pass from one person to the next.

Not just you, but us

Circulatory means flowing, and coming back around. In Eastern medicine, health is circulation: qi and blood moving through the body, the Five Elements turning into one another, the body in continuous exchange with its surroundings. When the flow stalls, care is what sets it moving again.

Circulatory Sensing builds on that. Here, sensing is not about you alone but about us. What one hand feels flows on to the next person and circles back around the group, so each person's state is met by someone else. The devices read the body as it is now, not as a fixed type, and answer at once: in light, in sound, in a buzz in the hand. In that circle the work looks for a kind of healing that happens between people: being noticed, and noticing back.

What the circulation awakens

  1. The body

    Attention to your own body: the pressure of your hold, your breath, your rhythm, felt the way an Eastern practitioner feels the pulse: as a flow, not a number.

  2. Each other

    Care and empathy. No device completes the effect alone, so people notice how the others are and adjust to them, without being told to.

  3. The surroundings

    Body and surroundings in correspondence. The light moves out into the room and the people in it, so sensing becomes a shared space rather than a private reading.

The terms

Eastern systems of caregiác hơi · 拔罐
The body-care knowledge of East and Southeast Asia: cupping, hand acupuncture, pulse and colour reading, and the Five Elements as a way of relating the body to its seasons, organs and surroundings. These are systems, not remedies. Each has its own way of reading the body and of responding to it, and they live in everyday practice: cupping is still done at home and in neighbourhood clinics across Ho Chi Minh City. Care here also follows Joan Tronto and María Puig de la Bellacasa: care as the ongoing maintenance of relations, and responsiveness to how others are.
CosmotechnicsYuk Hui
The philosopher Yuk Hui argues that technology is not one universal thing. Every culture's technics carry its own cosmology; he defines cosmotechnics as the unification of the cosmic and moral order through technical activities (The Question Concerning Technology in China, 2016). This research treats Eastern medicine as such a technics: the Five Elements are a working technical system, with their own logic of diagnosis and response, not a metaphor or a decorative theme.
Qi, blood and the channelskhí huyết · kinh lạc · 氣血 · 經絡
What circulates. In Eastern medicine, qi and blood move through a network of channels, the meridians, that run beneath the skin and link the inside of the body to its surface, and the body to its surroundings. Health is that movement; illness is where it stalls. Diagnosis follows the flow through four examinations (四診): looking, listening, asking and touching (Nan Jing, difficulty 61), with the pulse read under the fingers as the flow itself. Circulatory Sensing works at this level, with pressure, breath and rhythm, more than with the surface.
Skin-landthe threshold
Across East Asian traditions, body and environment are understood to be in continuous correspondence. The skin is the threshold where circulation crosses between them, and where it becomes visible and touchable: a cupping mark is circulation showing at the surface. The historian Shigehisa Kuriyama (The Expressiveness of the Body, 1999) describes how Chinese medicine read the body through what the surface gives away, the pulse under the fingers and the colour of the complexion. This research calls the relation skin-land: not the object of sensing, but the place where people, devices and surroundings meet.
The Five Elementsngũ hành · 五行
Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal and Water: five phases that generate one another in one cycle (tương sinh · 相生) and restrain one another in another (tương khắc · 相克). In this work they are the grammar for sequence and response, and for the light, sound and colour that answer: thumb to little finger, wood to water. They are used as a cultural coordinate system, not as medical advice.
Participatory, embodied interaction
People take part with their bodies: holding, pressing, breathing, rolling. Nobody is told what the devices mean or what to do. Whatever happens, happens between the participants, and that is what the research observes.

02The research

How can Eastern cosmological models of relation and care generate, and be enacted through, cultural interaction models?

  1. Design grammar. Which principles, drawn from Eastern diagnostic logic and the circulation it reads, produce touch-based sensing systems that bring people into relation without instruction?
  2. Care and responsiveness. How do such devices invite care between the people who meet through them, including strangers?
  3. Does the cosmology matter? Do the devices work differently with Five Elements logic than with an arbitrary colour mapping on the same hardware, and do they still invite care in public space without a facilitator?
01 · ALIGNMENT

The diagnosis is the grammar

Pressure, hold and breath map to Five Elements states, and the response follows the generating cycle. Reading and response happen together, as under a practitioner's fingers.

02 · RELATION

No device completes it alone

The effect only happens between people. Participants respond to one another, holding still, breathing and synchronising, rather than giving commands.

03 · MINIMAL COMPUTING

Small, local, self-sufficient

Each device runs on one microcontroller with no cloud. Browser studies run on an ordinary phone. The whole system works without institutional infrastructure.

How the research works

The research is carried out by making. Each making cycle builds a family of devices and tests it in short sessions with small groups. Participants are told only that they are invited to experience the space and the objects: nothing about the cosmology, the device logic or what is expected.

Each session is read against indicators set in advance: how closely participants' pressure and breath align, how long they stay and whether they come back, and unprompted care, such as adjusting to someone else or offering a turn. A comparative session with arbitrary colour mapping on the same hardware tests whether the cosmological logic matters.

05Oryun Lab

Where the work is made

Oryun holds two readings: 五輪, five rings, and 五倫, five rules. The lab's mark joins them: five petals drawn as one unbroken line that passes through a shared centre, held in an orbit.

Oryun Lab is Bin Youn's studio for devices, browser works and workshops built on the Five Elements. The mark comes in three lights: the studio's five in-between colours (間色), the research programme's meeting of Fire and Water, and the five source colours (五方色) of Circulation. See the brand.

Bin Youn is an artist and educator in Digital Media, School of Communication and Design, RMIT University Vietnam, in Ho Chi Minh City. The work draws on three years of hand acupuncture practice, in which the hand is read as a map of the whole body.
